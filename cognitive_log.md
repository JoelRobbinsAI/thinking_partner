To determine what should become part of long-term memory, focus on information that is **meaningfully processed, relevant, and deeply encoded**. Here’s a structured breakdown:

### 1. **Meaningful Connections**  
   - **Elaborative rehearsal**: Link new information to existing knowledge or create associations (e.g., analogies, mnemonics).  
   - **Conceptual understanding**: Prioritize understanding the "why" behind information rather than rote memorization.  

### 2. **Relevance and Purpose**  
   - **Personal significance**: Information tied to your goals, interests, or daily life is more likely to be retained.  
   - **Practical application**: Learn material that can be applied in real-world contexts (e.g., skills, problem-solving).  

### 3. **Repetition and Practice**  
   - **Spaced repetition**: Review information at intervals to strengthen memory traces (e.g., using flashcards or study schedules).  
   - **Active engagement**: Practice retrieving information through quizzes, teaching others, or problem-solving.  

### 4. **Emotional and Sensory Engagement**  
   - **Emotional impact**: Vivid or emotionally charged experiences (e.g., personal stories, surprises) are often remembered more vividly.  
   - **Multisensory input**: Use multiple senses (e.g., visual, auditory, kinesthetic) to enhance encoding.  

### 5. **Attention and Focus**  
   - **Deep processing**: Pay sustained attention to the material, avoiding distractions.  
   - **Prioritization**: Identify what is most critical to retain and focus mental resources on that.  

### 6. **Structure and Organization**  
   - **Chunking**: Group related information into manageable units (e.g., breaking down a phone number into segments).  
   - **Hierarchical organization**: Use frameworks like mind maps or categorization to structure knowledge.  

### 7. **Sleep and Consolidation**  
   - **Restorative sleep**: Allow time for memory consolidation during sleep, especially REM and slow-wave stages.  

### Example:  
If learning a new language, focus on:  
- **Meaningful phrases** (e.g., "How are you?" vs. random vocabulary),  
- **Practice using sentences** in context,  
- **Connecting words to emotions or experiences** (e.g., associating "happy" with a memorable event).  

By prioritizing these strategies, working memory processes information more effectively, increasing the likelihood of transferring it to long-term memory for retention and use.
